# Review framework

Use this for a deeper review or when prioritization is unclear. This is a reasoning aid, not empirical proof of an employer’s actual process.

## Build a compact role/evidence map

For each central requirement or responsibility, identify its exact source, priority (required, preferred, accepted alternative or inferred), CV evidence, what the evidence actually establishes, and the useful next move. Do not show a giant table if four rows explain the case. Preserve contribution scope: a group project does not mean the candidate designed every subsystem. A technology named in a skills list does not establish professional tenure.

## Pick the bottleneck, then the tactic

- Wording/retrieval: make supported role terms and aliases visible in context; do not keyword-dump unrelated tools.
- Buried evidence: move the strongest relevant accomplishment upward and state who used the work, what the candidate owned and what changed.
- Thin substance: propose a few broad kinds of experience or responsibility that would add the missing signal. Ask what already exists before assuming a new undertaking is needed.
- Unclear fit: connect transferable experience to the actual tasks rather than pretending the original role was the same job.
- Eligibility/application answers: distinguish a confirmed constraint from a question or unknown. A CV wording change does not alter an actual eligibility fact.
- Extraction: inspect text order and completeness; do not confuse text missing after extraction with a candidate omission.
- Unknown screening route: recommend clarity useful to people and retrieval; do not diagnose a rejection from its speed or the ATS URL.

Rank by relevance to the central work, available candidate evidence and effort. Label quick edits versus larger additions; avoid a fake precision score. More projects are not automatically the answer for someone whose existing work is already strong.

## Turn a hiring report into a candidate action

Use the linked synthesis and ledger. For each consequential claim ask: Who reports this? What did they actually observe? Is it first-hand? Is the role/company scope relevant? What competing account exists? Which part is our inference? What can the candidate do even if the hypothesized screening mechanism is absent?

For example, reported heavy inbound volume can support making relevance easier to scan. It cannot establish a universal review duration or that an individual CV was never opened. A report of an Ashby recruiter using AI and another barely using it supports uncertainty about adoption, not choosing one as the universal truth.

Keep self-described insiders labeled. Prefer dated, attributable observations over “everyone knows” advice. A comment’s score is not evidence quality; repeated retellings are not independent sources. Source counts do not estimate prevalence.

## Make general suggestions substantial

A useful broad recommendation has a role signal, a reason that signal matters, a few possible directions and a CV placement or draft line where helpful. It does not need a prescribed gadget, stack, event, timeline or acceptance test. Develop those only after the candidate chooses a direction.

Surface real but undersold experience across work, study, personal and community contexts. Match the label to the context. Distinguish current facts, things to confirm/develop and explicitly requested hypothetical claims. Explain hypothetical provenance once per relevant group, not a warning under every sentence. Do not assert that a claim is undetectable, that a source proves how one employer checks it, or that a particular tactic guarantees an interview.

## Depth and follow-up

A quick review can lead with three strong moves. A deep review should explain the evidence and hiring rationale behind four to six priorities, with short source notes only where they change the advice. Cut generic slogans and repeated limitations. End with the smallest useful set of questions. When answered, update the selected wording and priorities rather than repeat the full essay.
